Federal Budget, Parliamentary Amendments & Budget Execution

The federal government spends twenty times more on debt interest than on Bolsa Família, and R$ 40 billion in earmarks went out with no identified recipient.

Budget execution by managing unit, published by the National Treasury, parliamentary earmarks registered by the Office of the Comptroller General, tax revenue by state from the Federal Revenue Service, and rural credit operations from the Central Bank together form the most complete portrait available of how federal public money is decided, collected and actually spent. That portrait shows a budget where a large share of what's authorized never becomes delivered service, a relevant slice goes with no trace of a recipient at all, and debt service alone consumes twenty times more resources than the country's main cash-transfer program.

Total parliamentary earmarks (2022-2025)

Between 2022 and 2025, Congress committed R$ 152.7 billion across 25,518 parliamentary earmarks, averaging R$ 5.98 million per earmark. That is almost what Bolsa Família paid in a full year (R$ 163.5 billion in 2024) — except decided by lawmakers, committees and state caucuses, without the targeting logic toward those who need it most that shapes the design of a social program. In 2026, through September, another R$ 40.1 billion has been committed.

Budget execution: how much becomes real spending?

In none of the years analyzed did the budget execution rate come close to 100%. In 2019, only 44% of what was committed became liquidated spending; even in 2024, the series' best-performing year, 30% still went without full execution. The gap between what's promised and what's delivered isn't a technical detail — it's the distance between the political discourse of the budget and its actual delivery to the population.

General Rapporteur's earmarks: extreme concentration

In 2022, the last year they existed, the Budget general rapporteur's earmarks reached R$ 8.6 billion committed, directed mostly toward health; between 2020 and 2022 they totaled R$ 46.6 billion. It's a concentration of decision-making power that escapes the usual process of parliamentary dispute spread across hundreds of representatives and senators, concentrating in a single role — the rapporteur — the capacity to decide where billions of reais will be spent.

Sectoral concentration of earmarks

More than half of all parliamentary earmark value, 51.8%, goes to the Health function — R$ 79.2 billion. Public Security, with 1.4% of the total, and Social Assistance, with 2.6%, are left with residual fractions against that amount. The sectoral concentration of earmarks reveals where lawmakers concentrate electoral bargaining power, which doesn't always coincide with where social need is greatest.

Tax structure: companies vs. workers

Corporate income tax (IRPJ), levied on company profits, raised R$ 315.2 billion in 2022, against R$ 57.9 billion for personal income tax (IRPF), levied on individual income — a ratio of more than five to one. That doesn't by itself mean the Brazilian tax system is progressive: a relevant part of total revenue still comes from taxes on consumption, which weigh proportionally more on those who earn less, and don't show up in this specific breakdown.

Unpaid carryovers: the government's hidden debt

In 2020, the government registered R$ 290 billion in unpaid carryovers ("restos a pagar") — resources committed but not liquidated within the fiscal year, which remain available for use in future years without new budget approval. Every year, a fraction of this stock — between R$ 8 billion and R$ 15 billion — is simply canceled, money that had already been committed on paper but never turned into service delivered to the population.

Discretionary spending: what gets cut

Education and Health, politically protected functions, kept execution rates of 85% and 90% respectively of their allocations. Science, with just 65% executed, and Environment, with 55%, are systematically the first targets for spending freezes when the government needs to cut spending during the fiscal year — a pattern that penalizes precisely the areas of investment with slower, less visible short-term returns.

Public debt: debt service

The federal government spends R$ 700 billion a year just on public-debt interest and charges — twenty times more than the R$ 35 billion a year earmarked for Bolsa Família. This imbalance between the cost of public debt and investment in cash transfers shows how much debt service dominates the fiscal space available for social policy.

Tax burden: composition

Indirect taxes — such as ICMS and IPI, charged on consumption — account for 55% of the entire Brazilian tax burden. Direct taxes, like income tax and CSLL, hold 35%, and wealth taxes, which could directly target large fortunes, hold less than 5% of total revenue. Since consumption tax applies equally to rich and poor in nominal terms, it weighs proportionally more on those who earn less.

Secret budget: spending with no trace

Between 2020 and 2022, R$ 40 billion in earmarks were executed with no clear identification of recipient — the so-called "secret budget." Without knowing who received what, any real oversight becomes impossible, whether by the press, control bodies, or civil society itself. It's the most opaque point in the entire federal budget cycle.

After the secret budget, committee earmarks

The Federal Supreme Court declared rapporteur earmarks unconstitutional at the end of 2022, and they disappear from the records from 2023 on. The money, however, did not return to the Executive: total committed earmarks rose from R$ 25.5 billion in 2022 to R$ 47.1 billion in 2025, the highest in the series. The rapporteur's space was taken over by committee earmarks — residual until 2022 and R$ 11.7 billion in 2024 — and by individual earmarks, which more than doubled.

Special transfers — the so-called "Pix earmarks", which land directly in state and municipal coffers with no defined purpose — went from R$ 0.6 billion in 2020 to about R$ 7 billion a year since 2023. With them and with committee earmarks, whose individual sponsor does not appear in the record, a growing share of the money remains hard to trace back to whoever directed it.

The end of the secret budget did not shrink earmarks: they nearly doubled between 2022 and 2025, and the rapporteur's space passed to the committees.

Explanatory hypotheses

The low execution rate can be explained by the hypothesis of the budget as a political bargaining chip: officials commit resources to show action to voters, without any real commitment to short-term execution. The concentration of decision-making power in few hands — general rapporteur, specific committees — reveals a captured Legislature, in which a reduced number of actors control a disproportionate share of budget allocation. The regressive tax structure overall reflects a captured State, in which capital influences the rules to reduce its effective burden. And the secret budget represents the most extreme form of that capture: public money moved with no accountability whatsoever.

Policy implications

Expanding proactive transparency about budget execution allows direct scrutiny by civil society and the press. Tying earmark value to actual execution — treating unpaid carryovers as a control indicator, not a discretionary reserve — tends to improve public-policy delivery. Advancing tax progressivity, with greater taxation on wealth and large fortunes, could correct part of the distortion that today makes the system weigh more on those who earn less. Fully abolishing any remnant of budget with no recipient identification would restore a minimum of democratic accountability. Reducing the cost of public debt would free up a significant portion of the R$ 700 billion currently allocated to interest for social policy. And reinvesting in science and the environment — areas among the first to be cut today — would help restore long-term state capacities that the country continues to neglect.
