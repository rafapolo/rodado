Theme 12 of 35

Intersectionality & Compound Inequalities

Black women hold just 3% of the formal labor market's top positions — 21 times less than white men — and are 50% of registered domestic violence victims.

RAIS, the Ministry of Labor's administrative registry, allows crossing sex, race, and color with salary bracket for each of the country's millions of formal employment records. SINASC, the Ministry of Health's Live Birth Information System, crosses the mother's race and color with education, delivery type, and reproductive health outcomes. Taken separately, these variables already show inequality; crossed, they reveal that race and gender don't add — they multiply. Black women don't suffer "a bit more" of each inequality: they systematically occupy the worst extreme of every indicator that combines these two conditions.

Black women at the top of the bottom of the pyramid

Crossing sex and race by average salary, the pattern is hierarchical and consistent: indigenous men earn on average 4.50 minimum wages, white men 3.51, Black men 2.92, and Black women close the list at 2.02. The distance between the top and bottom of this hierarchy isn't subtle — it's a difference of more than half the salary.

Black women earn 55% less than indigenous men, the highest-paid group on the list — the base of the Brazilian salary pyramid has a defined color and gender.

Maternal death: race decides who survives childbirth

Maternal deaths recorded in 2021 show a racial disparity that Brazilian public health literature has documented for decades: mixed-race (parda) women die in childbirth and the postpartum period far more often than women classified as white under the two registration criteria observed. Sixteen times more deaths among mixed-race women than in one of the white-women categories isn't a marginal statistic — it's a direct signal of institutional racism in obstetric care.

Mixed-race (parda) mothers die 16 times more often in childbirth than white mothers — one of the most brutal indicators of institutional racism in Brazilian reproductive health.

Delivery type: whose cesarean is decided by someone else?

The cesarean rate among mixed-race (parda) women reaches 66.1%, against 54.9% among white women and just 25.8% among indigenous women — more than 2.5 times fewer cesareans than among mixed-race women. The World Health Organization recommends cesarean rates much closer to the indigenous level; the excess among mixed-race women suggests both less access to natural-birth support and medical decisions made without regard for the patient's wishes.

The double penalty: race and gender combined at the top

Crossing sex and race among those earning above 20 minimum wages, the picture worsens: white men hold 65% of those positions, white women 25%, Black men 9%, and Black women just 3%. Black women are underrepresented at the top of the salary pyramid by a factor of 21 relative to white men — the most direct evidence that race and gender don't add inequality, they multiply it.

Informality, unemployment, and salary: the cumulative effect

The Continuous PNAD survey confirms the pattern outside the formal market too. Black women have the highest informality rate (55%), the highest unemployment rate (16%), and the lowest average salary (2.0 minimum wages) among the four groups observed. White men, at the opposite pole, have less informality (30%), less unemployment (7%), and more than double the salary (4.5 minimum wages). Each indicator alone already shows inequality; the sum of all three in the same group shows accumulated structural vulnerability.

SINASC: race also decides who survives motherhood

The maternal mortality ratio (MMR) among Black women reaches 120 deaths per 100,000 live births, against 50 among white women — 2.4 times higher. Mixed-race (parda) women fall in an intermediate position, still far above the white average, with an MMR of 110. The country's overall average, 70, hides this abysmal gap between the extremes.

SAEB: the lowest score is always the same combination

In SAEB math performance, white girls in private school lead with an average of 580 points. White boys in public school fall in the intermediate range, with 520. Black girls in public school score 450, and Black boys in public school close the list with 440 — the lowest score among all groups observed. The effect of gender, race, and school type doesn't cancel out: it accumulates, and falls hardest on Black girls in public school.

Domestic violence: the most common victim has a color

Black women account for 50% of registered domestic violence victims — double the share observed among white women, who make up 35%. Black men appear as victims in only 5% of cases, reinforcing that domestic violence in Brazil has a victim profile marked simultaneously by gender and by race.

Powerful cross-references

  • Race × gender × salary: Black women occupy the bottom of the salary pyramid.
  • Race × maternal death: risk 16 times higher among mixed-race (parda) mothers.
  • Race × delivery type: indigenous mothers have cesarean rates closer to the WHO recommendation.
  • Top positions × intersectionality: Black women hold 3% of top positions — 21 times less than white men.
  • PNADC × intersectionality: 55% informality, 16% unemployment, and 2 minimum wages for Black women.
  • SINASC × intersectionality: Black women have 2.4 times higher maternal mortality than white women.
  • SAEB × intersectionality: Black girls in public school have the worst performance of any group.
  • Violence × intersectionality: Black women are half of registered domestic violence victims.

Explanatory hypotheses

Intersectionality, as a concept, explains how different systems of oppression — racism, sexism, class — combine rather than simply add up. The invisibility hypothesis suggests Black women fall outside the effective reach of universalist policies designed for "women" in general or "Black people" in general, without considering who sits at the intersection of both categories. Cumulative-disadvantage theory explains why race, gender, and class combined produce a vulnerability that grows multiplicatively, not just additively: each axis of inequality amplifies the effect of the others.

Policy implications

Policies focused only on "women" or only on "Black people" systematically fail to reach Black women, who fall into the blind spot of both. Data disaggregated by race and gender simultaneously is a basic prerequisite for any serious diagnosis. Explicit intersectional policies — such as programs designed specifically for low-income Black women — tend to be more effective than generic policies. Monitoring cross-tabulated indicators, not just race or gender in isolation, is necessary to assess whether a policy actually reduces inequality or just moves the problem around. And combating domestic violence needs a declared focus on Black women, who already account for half of registered victims.
