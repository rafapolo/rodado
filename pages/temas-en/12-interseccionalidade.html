Intersectionality & Compound Inequalities

Black women are 2.2 times more likely than white women to die from pregnancy, and a formally employed white man is 12 times more likely than a Black woman to earn more than 20 minimum wages.

RAIS, the Ministry of Labor's administrative registry, allows crossing sex, race, and color with salary bracket for each of the country's millions of formal employment records. SINASC, the Ministry of Health's Live Birth Information System, crosses the mother's race and color with education, delivery type, and reproductive health outcomes. Taken separately, these variables already show inequality; crossed, they reveal that race and gender don't add — they multiply. Black women don't suffer "a bit more" of each inequality: they systematically occupy the worst extreme of every indicator that combines these two conditions.

Black women at the base of the wage pyramid

Among formal jobs active on December 31, 2025 recorded in RAIS, white men earn on average R$ 5,503 a month. White women earn R$ 4,638; pardo (mixed-race) men, R$ 3,652; Black men, R$ 3,624. Black and pardo women close the list: R$ 3,279 and R$ 3,234 — 59% of the white male average. The median, less sensitive to very high salaries, shows the same order with a smaller gap: R$ 3,206 for white men and R$ 2,171 for pardo women.

Maternal death: Black women die twice as often

Crossing maternal deaths from the Mortality Information System with births from SINASC, both from the Ministry of Health, the 2022 maternal mortality ratio was 102.6 deaths per 100 thousand live births among Black women, against 46.8 among white women — 2.2 times higher. Indigenous women stand at 75.3. Pardo women, at 51.9, are close to white women. The pattern predates the pandemic: in 2019, 103.4 among Black women and 48.7 among white women.

Delivery: c-sections follow color and income

Read with SINASC's official dictionary, 2022 births show the highest c-section rate among white mothers (66.1%), followed by pardo (54.9%) and Black mothers (51.4%). Indigenous mothers stand at 25.8%, the only group close to what the World Health Organization considers justified. The ranking follows access to scheduled delivery in private care — and the previous version of this page swapped the white and pardo labels.

Who reaches the top of the formal labor market?

In the 2025 RAIS, 357 thousand jobs held by white, Black, pardo and Indigenous men and women paid more than 20 minimum wages a month (R$ 30,360). White men hold 54% of these positions, although they are 24% of jobs. Black and pardo women add up to 23% of jobs and 5.6% of the top. Proportionally, a formally employed white man is 12 times more likely to earn above 20 minimum wages than a Black woman.

Informality, unemployment, and salary: the cumulative effect

The Continuous PNAD survey confirms the pattern outside the formal market too. Black women have the highest informality rate (55%), the highest unemployment rate (16%), and the lowest average salary (2.0 minimum wages) among the four groups observed. White men, at the opposite pole, have less informality (30%), less unemployment (7%), and more than double the salary (4.5 minimum wages). Each indicator alone already shows inequality; the sum of all three in the same group shows accumulated structural vulnerability.

SAEB: the lowest score is always the same combination

In SAEB math performance, white girls in private school lead with an average of 580 points. White boys in public school fall in the intermediate range, with 520. Black girls in public school score 450, and Black boys in public school close the list with 440 — the lowest score among all groups observed. The effect of gender, race, and school type doesn't cancel out: it accumulates, and falls hardest on Black girls in public school.

Domestic violence: the most common victim has a color

Black women account for 50% of registered domestic violence victims — double the share observed among white women, who make up 35%. Black men appear as victims in only 5% of cases, reinforcing that domestic violence in Brazil has a victim profile marked simultaneously by gender and by race.

Explanatory hypotheses

Intersectionality, as a concept, explains how different systems of oppression — racism, sexism, class — combine rather than simply add up. The invisibility hypothesis suggests Black women fall outside the effective reach of universalist policies designed for "women" in general or "Black people" in general, without considering who sits at the intersection of both categories. Cumulative-disadvantage theory explains why race, gender, and class combined produce a vulnerability that grows multiplicatively, not just additively: each axis of inequality amplifies the effect of the others.

Policy implications

Policies focused only on "women" or only on "Black people" systematically fail to reach Black women, who fall into the blind spot of both. Data disaggregated by race and gender simultaneously is a basic prerequisite for any serious diagnosis. Explicit intersectional policies — such as programs designed specifically for low-income Black women — tend to be more effective than generic policies. Monitoring cross-tabulated indicators, not just race or gender in isolation, is necessary to assess whether a policy actually reduces inequality or just moves the problem around. And combating domestic violence needs a declared focus on Black women, who already account for half of registered victims.
