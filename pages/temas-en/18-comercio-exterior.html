Foreign Trade, Global Integration & Value Chains

In 2025 Brazil exported 869 million tonnes at US$ 404 per tonne and imported 188 million at US$ 1,497. The US$ 70 billion surplus sits alongside a US$ 75 billion deficit in machinery, electronics and vehicles.

Comex Stat, from the Ministry of Development, Industry, Trade and Services, records every good that enters or leaves the country, with product code, country of origin or destination, weight and dollar value. The 2025 figures show an export basket of primary products concentrated on a single buyer, China, and an import basket of industrial goods worth almost four times as much per tonne.

Trade balance: a US$ 70 billion surplus

In 2025 exports totaled US$ 351.2 billion and imports US$ 281.3 billion. The US$ 69.9 billion surplus is smaller than in 2024 (US$ 77.3 billion) because imports grew 7%, faster than exports (3%).

The surplus comes from primary products and pays for the industrial deficit. When commodity prices fall, the balance shrinks with them.

The basket: oil, soy and iron ore are a third of everything

Three unprocessed products, crude oil (US$ 45.1 billion), soybeans (US$ 43.8 billion) and iron ore (US$ 28.8 billion), accounted for 33.5% of 2025 exports. Adding coffee, beef, sugar, pulp, chicken, soybean meal, corn and cotton, the eleven main primary products reach 57.2% of the basket.

China: 29% of exports, 87% of them in five products

China bought US$ 100.9 billion from Brazil in 2025, 28.7% of exports, nearly three times what the United States bought (US$ 37.6 billion, 10.7%). On the import side, China sold US$ 71.0 billion (25.2%). The balance with China was a US$ 29.9 billion surplus; with the United States, a US$ 7.7 billion deficit. Five products (soy, oil, iron ore, beef and pulp) make up 87.1% of everything China buys from Brazil.

Value per tonne: what comes in is worth 3.7 times what goes out

In 2025 Brazil shipped 869 million tonnes of goods at an average of US$ 404 per tonne and received 188 million tonnes at US$ 1,497. An imported tonne is worth 3.7 times an exported one. It is the simplest way to gauge the technological content of the basket: the country ships volume and buys value.

Industry: a US$ 75 billion deficit in machinery, electronics and vehicles

Mechanical machinery and equipment led 2025 imports with US$ 47.4 billion (16.8%), followed by fuels (US$ 30.6 billion), electrical and electronic equipment (US$ 30.2 billion), vehicles (US$ 23.5 billion) and fertilizers (US$ 15.5 billion). In the five chapters of industrial goods with the highest technological content (machinery, electronics, vehicles, aircraft and instruments), Brazil imported US$ 115.2 billion and exported US$ 40.2 billion: a US$ 75.0 billion deficit, larger than the entire trade surplus.

Powerful crossings

  • Commodities × basket: oil, soy and iron ore are 33.5% of 2025 exports.
  • China × concentration: 28.7% of exports go to China, 87% of them in five products.
  • Value × tonne: an imported tonne is worth 3.7 times an exported one.
  • Industry × deficit: a US$ 75 bn deficit in machinery, electronics, vehicles, aircraft and instruments.
  • Soy × fertilizer: US$ 15.5 bn of imported fertilizer against US$ 43.8 bn of soybean exports.
  • US × balance: a US$ 29.9 bn surplus with China and a US$ 7.7 bn deficit with the United States.

Explanatory hypotheses

The theory of deteriorating terms of trade, from Prebisch and ECLAC, describes the trap: countries that export raw materials and import manufactures must sell ever more volume to buy the same amount of industrial goods. Trade liberalization in the 1990s, the strong currency during the commodity boom and Chinese demand reinforced specialization in primary products, where Brazil has a natural advantage, while manufacturing lost weight. Dependence on imported fertilizers shows that even the agricultural advantage rests on inputs from abroad.

Policy implications

Diversifying the basket requires industrial policy tied to the commodity chains themselves: soy crushing, steel, higher-value pulp, refining and chemicals. Trade agreements that reduce tariff escalation on processed goods, applied by several importers, would widen room to sell meal, oil and meat instead of beans. Reducing dependence on imported fertilizer is a matter of production security. And concentration on a single buyer argues for opening markets in Asia, the Middle East and Africa for the same products.
