Political Economy & Development

In 2023 the Federal Revenue Service withheld R$ 246 billion in income tax from labor and R$ 122 billion from financial investments, while the rural land tax raised R$ 3.2 billion. In the states, payroll takes 53% of spending and investment 6%.

Brazil's Federal Revenue Service (Receita Federal) publishes, month by month and state by state, how much each tax raises: personal and corporate income tax, withholding on wages, investments and remittances abroad, contributions on turnover and the rural land tax. The National Treasury's SICONFI shows how each state spends, and its Payment Capacity rating (CAPAG) grades each one's fiscal health. Together, the three sources describe a state that taxes mainly consumption and wages, barely taxes wealth and, at the state level, devotes most of its budget to payroll.

Income tax: wages pay more than capital

In 2023 income tax withheld at source on wages totaled R$ 187.6 billion. With another R$ 58.6 billion from individuals' annual returns, labor income accounted for R$ 246 billion. Withholding on capital income (interest, investments and funds) came to R$ 121.7 billion, half as much. Profits and dividends paid to individuals were tax-exempt from 1996 until the reform approved in 2025. Companies paid R$ 300.3 billion in corporate income tax (IRPJ) and R$ 156.8 billion in the social contribution on profits (CSLL), but the income that reaches shareholders as dividends appears in no line of this table.

Consumption versus wealth: R$ 552 billion against R$ 3 billion

Federal taxes and contributions on turnover and consumption (COFINS, PIS/PASEP, IPI and IOF) raised R$ 551.6 billion in 2023, built into the price of everything people buy. The only federal tax on wealth, the Rural Land Tax (ITR), raised R$ 3.2 billion. That is less than 0.6% of the take from consumption. Inheritance and urban property are taxed by states and municipalities, and the tax on large fortunes written into the 1988 Constitution has never been enacted.

São Paulo pays 36% of personal income tax and produces 31% of GDP

In 2023 taxpayers in São Paulo paid R$ 21.1 billion in personal income tax, 36% of the national total. Minas Gerais (10.1%) and Rio de Janeiro (9.7%) follow, each with less than a third of that. São Paulo's share of personal income tax exceeds its share of GDP, which IBGE puts at 31.5% of the R$ 10.9 trillion of 2023. The gap shows that high taxable income, from professionals, executives and people declaring large gains, is even more concentrated than production.

States: 53% of spending goes to payroll, 6% to investment

In 2024 the 27 state governments recorded R$ 1.37 trillion in spending. R$ 726 billion (53.1%) went to personnel and related charges, including retirees and pensioners, and R$ 84 billion (6.2%) to investment. In Rio Grande do Norte payroll takes 72.9% of spending, in Rio Grande do Sul 69.3% and in Minas Gerais 66.4%; in those three states investment is between 2% and 5%. At the other end, Amazonas (39.5%) and São Paulo (40.8%) commit less than 41%.

CAPAG: the states most tied to payroll get the worst ratings

The National Treasury's Payment Capacity rating grades states from A+ to D on indebtedness, current savings and liquidity; only states rated A or B can borrow with a federal guarantee. In the 2025 assessment, 21 of the 27 states scored between A+ and B. The six rated C or D are Acre, the Federal District, Rio Grande do Norte and Rio Grande do Sul (C), and Minas Gerais and Rio de Janeiro (D). The three states with the largest share of spending tied to payroll (RN, RS and MG) are all in that group.

Powerful crossings

  • Labor × capital: R$ 246 bn of income tax on wages and annual returns against R$ 122 bn on financial investments (2023).
  • Consumption × wealth: R$ 552 bn from COFINS, PIS, IPI and IOF against R$ 3.2 bn from the rural land tax.
  • Dividends × exemption: profits paid to shareholders were outside income tax from 1996 until the 2025 reform.
  • São Paulo × high income: 31.5% of GDP and 36% of personal income tax.
  • Payroll × investment: states spent 53% on personnel and 6% on investment in 2024.
  • Payroll × CAPAG: RN, RS and MG, with over 66% of spending on personnel, are rated C or D.

Explanatory hypotheses

Brazil's tax structure reflects the political economy of how it was built. Consumption taxes and withholding at source are easy to collect and invisible to those who pay; taxing wealth and dividends means confronting those with the loudest voice in Congress. That is why the system weighs proportionally more on those who earn less. In the states, payroll rigidity, with job tenure, earmarked spending and loss-making civil service pension schemes, explains why personnel costs crowd out investment whenever revenue shrinks. A low CAPAG rating closes the loop: without access to guaranteed credit, the most indebted states also lose the means to invest.

Policy implications

The dividend tax approved in 2025 and the consumption tax reform, which in 2026 begins moving PIS, COFINS, IPI, ICMS and ISS into two value-added taxes, are the first structural changes in decades, and their effect should be measured in the Revenue Service's own series. Updating the land values declared for the rural land tax, which are self-reported and outdated, would raise revenue without creating a new tax. In the states, civil service pension reforms and limits on new mandatory spending are a condition for freeing room for investment, and CAPAG can be used to tie federal support to payroll targets.
