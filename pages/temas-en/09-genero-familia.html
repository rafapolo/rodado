Gender, Family & Demographic Dynamics

121,027 girls under 18 had children in Brazil in 2024 — more than 330 a day —, and among Indigenous mothers more than one in seven was a teenager.

The Live Birth Information System (SINASC), run by the Ministry of Health, records practically every birth in the country — mother's age, race, education, week of gestation, and type of delivery. CAGED, the General Register of Employed and Unemployed Persons, completes the picture from the other side of adult life, showing how the labor market treats men and women unequally. Cross-referenced, the two panels show a Brazil where gender organizes who gets pregnant too young, who is operated on during delivery, who works formally, and who suffers violence at home.

Teenage mothers: 121,027 children having children

Brazil has one of the highest teenage-pregnancy rates in the world, and the absolute numbers are more striking than any percentage: more than 121,000 children under 18 had children in a single year, at an average age of just 16. São Paulo leads in absolute numbers, but states in the North and Northeast follow close behind, revealing that the phenomenon spans the entire country, not a single region.

Teenage mothers by race: Indigenous and pardo mothers lead

Read with the official dictionary of the Live Birth Information System — where code 1 is white and code 4 is pardo (mixed-race) —, the 2024 records show, like those of 2022, the opposite of what this page used to say: pardo mothers account for 82,058 of the 121,027 births to mothers under 18, 68% of the total. The difference is not just population size. Among pardo mothers, 6.2% of births are to teenagers; among white mothers, 3.0%; among Indigenous mothers, 15.0% — more than one in seven.

C-sections by race: white mothers at the top

The highest c-section rate is among white mothers: 67.3% of 2024 births with a recorded delivery type, more than four times the 10% to 15% the World Health Organization considers justified. Pardo (58.2%) and Black (54.8%) mothers follow, and Indigenous mothers, at 29.3%, are closest to the recommendation. The ranking follows access to scheduled delivery in private care, not differences between pregnancies.

2022 Census: how many children each woman had

The 2022 Demographic Census asked every woman aged 12 or older how many live-born children she had. Among women aged 45 to 49, who have practically completed their reproductive lives, the average is 2.01 children — 1.78 among white women, 2.20 among pardo women, 2.13 among Black women and 3.61 among Indigenous women. Among those aged 15 to 19 the gap appears early: 0.09 children per woman among white women, 0.15 among pardo and Black women and 0.35 among Indigenous women.

PNADC: female labor-market participation

Women participate in the labor force at a rate twenty percentage points lower than men. And when they do enter the market, they face higher unemployment and informality rates. It's a double disadvantage: fewer women work, and those who do work under worse conditions.

Paid domestic work: racialized and invisible

Paid domestic work is, at once, one of the country's largest employers and one of its most precarious. Most workers are Black, most have no signed work permit, and the average pay sits well below other occupations. It's the most racialized occupation in the Brazilian labor market — and also one of the least protected.

Single mothers: concentration by class and race

Single motherhood isn't distributed evenly across classes and races: low-income Black women account for more than half of cases, against a small fraction among high-income white women. It's exactly the group with the fewest financial resources that most often raises children alone, without a second income or a second support network at home.

Domestic violence: the victim profile

SINAN records on domestic violence draw a clear profile: the overwhelming majority of victims are women, the overwhelming majority of aggressors are men, and the crime happens mostly within already-established family ties. It isn't random violence from strangers — it's violence from partners and relatives, concentrated among women aged 20 to 40 with lower education.

Explanatory hypotheses

Teenage pregnancy can be explained by the combination of a lack of sex education in schools and limited access to contraception. The connection to inequality shows that more vulnerable families have less access to information and reproductive-health services, which raises the risk of early pregnancy. The gender gap in the labor market reflects the double-shift phenomenon: paid work added to unpaid domestic work, which falls disproportionately on women. And domestic violence as the predominant form of violence reveals that, for many Brazilian women, home remains the most dangerous place in their lives.

Policy implications

Effective sex-education programs in schools could measurably reduce teenage pregnancy. Access to contraception needs to be expanded, especially in the regions where early pregnancy is most common. Strengthening young women's economic autonomy has the potential to break the cycle of poverty that often accompanies early motherhood. Universal public daycare could increase women's participation in the formal labor market. Extended paternity leave could help redistribute the burden of domestic work between men and women. And a specific benefit for single mothers could reduce the vulnerability of female-headed single-parent families, currently concentrated among the country's poorest.
