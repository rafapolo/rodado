Health, Service Access & Social Determinants

White mothers deliver 67% of their babies by C-section, against 29% of Indigenous mothers — and only one in four Brazilians has health insurance, from 41% in São Paulo to 4% in Roraima.

The records from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC), run by the Ministry of Health, track practically every birth in the country — type of delivery, mother's race, education, age, and the baby's weight. The Mortality Information System (SIM) does the same at the other end of life, recording cause of death, age, and race for every death. And the Bolsa Família payments, audited by the Office of the Comptroller General (CGU), show how much — and to whom — the country's main cash-transfer program actually reaches. Together, the three panels sketch a Brazil in which being born, falling ill, and dying depend decisively on where and who you are.

C-sections: the national shame

Brazil has one of the highest C-section rates in the world, far above the 10% to 15% the World Health Organization considers medically justifiable — and the rate follows access, not risk. In 2024, white mothers had 67% of their babies by C-section; mixed-race mothers, 58%; Black mothers, 55%. Indigenous mothers, at the other end, stood at 29%. Because scheduled C-sections are far more common in private care, the gradient by color mostly reflects who has health insurance and a private doctor. (An earlier version of this page swapped SINASC's race codes and attributed white mothers' rate to mixed-race mothers.)

Firearms: eight in ten victims are Black

An earlier version of this page read SIM's race codes swapped — code 4 is mixed-race (parda), not white — and concluded that white people died more from firearms than mixed-race people. The correct reading shows the opposite: in 2024, of 27,423 firearm homicides, 79% killed Black or mixed-race people, and 19% white people. Black victims also die younger: an average age of 30, against 33 among white victims.

Bolsa Família: how much reaches the poor?

The new Bolsa Família, which replaced the old program in 2023, reaches all Brazilian municipalities — complete territorial coverage, no exceptions. The average amount per family, R$ 677 in September 2026, is more than three times the R$ 190 per payment of 2021, but it remains an income supplement: the program guarantees a presence across the entire territory, but doesn't guarantee, on its own, an exit from poverty.

Infant mortality: North/Northeast versus South/Southeast

The geography of infant mortality reproduces the map of regional inequality. In 2024, for every thousand live births, 14.5 babies in the North and 12.3 in the Northeast died before their first birthday, against 9.5 in the South — a 53% higher risk for those born in the North. The rate matches deaths of children under one from SIM with births from SINASC, by the mother's municipality of residence; without correction for underregistration, it tends to underestimate precisely the poorest regions.

CNES: doctors per thousand residents — the health deserts

Records from the National Registry of Health Establishments (CNES) show that access to doctors in Brazil is, above all, a matter of geography. The Southeast has nearly three doctors per thousand residents; the North has just over one. The gap between the two regions is more than two and a half to one — enough to turn an entire region of the country into a health desert.

Communicable diseases: leptospirosis, dengue, and zika

The diseases most dependent on basic sanitation and urban infrastructure — dengue, leptospirosis, zika — remain concentrated in exactly the regions where that infrastructure is most precarious. They are diseases of poverty in the most literal sense: they appear wherever sewage networks, regular garbage collection, and flood control are lacking.

SIA/SIH: medium- and high-complexity procedures

Procedures that require expensive equipment and specialized teams — CT scans, chemotherapy — are heavily concentrated in the capitals and major centers of the Southeast. For a patient from the interior, that means traveling, staying away from home, and often waiting weeks for an opening: an extra cost, financial and physical, on top of the treatment itself.

Birth weight: babies of vulnerable mothers

Birth weight works as an early portrait of a lifetime of health, and it's already unequal at birth. Children of mothers with less than four years of schooling or with no prenatal care at all have low-birth-weight rates well above the national average. It's the mother's vulnerability, transmitted even before delivery, determining the child's health odds.

Health insurance: one in four, and almost all in the Southeast

Data from the National Supplementary Health Agency (ANS) show who can turn to private care. In October 2025, 53.3 million people had a medical-hospital plan — 25% of the population estimated by IBGE. Coverage ranges from 40.6% in São Paulo to 4.4% in Roraima, a ninefold gap, and no state in the North or Northeast exceeds 19%. For three in four Brazilians, the public health system (SUS) is the only door.

Explanatory hypotheses

The high c-section rate can be explained by the convenience hypothesis: doctors and maternity wards prefer schedulable procedures that fit a professional agenda better than a vaginal birth of unpredictable duration. The connection to race suggests that white women, with more access to health plans and trusted doctors, have more autonomy to negotiate their own delivery — while mixed-race women, more dependent on the public health system (SUS), get less say in their preferences. Medicalization theory explains the backdrop: a hospital-centered model that prioritizes intervention over accompaniment. Regional inequality in mortality, meanwhile, reflects a pattern of internal colonialism, in which the country's peripheries remain structurally dependent on the center for the most complex services.

Policy implications

Stricter regulation of elective c-sections — with auditing of medical indication — could reduce a significant share of these rates. Support from doulas and obstetric nurses could humanize childbirth and restore women's agency in the process. Expanding the average Bolsa Família value is a necessary condition for the program to stop being merely a safety net and start lifting families out of extreme poverty. Bringing doctors to the interior — with more medical-school slots in the North and Northeast and mandatory placement in underserved regions — could reduce the health deserts identified in the CNES data. And effective universal prenatal care has the potential to cut low-birth-weight cases in half.
