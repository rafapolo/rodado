Atlas, Georeferenced Maps & Territorial Bases

Brazil formally protects 25% of its own territory in conservation units and Indigenous lands — but the Cerrado has already lost more forest than the Amazon.

The geobr project provides the official geometry of Indigenous lands, conservation units, biomes, and urban concentrations across Brazil, detailing ethnicity, regularization stage, protection category, and management level for each area. IBGE's 2022 Census rounds out that map with the geometry and population of Indigenous lands and Quilombola territories, while administrative divisions — mesoregions, microregions, municipalities, and census tracts — complete the country's territorial mesh down to the finest granularity available. Cross-referencing these layers, the picture that emerges is of a country with one of the world's largest formal networks of environmental protection, but where the geometry on paper doesn't always match effective protection on the ground.

Brazil has one of the world's largest territorial-protection networks

Adding up conservation units and Indigenous lands, a quarter of all Brazilian territory sits under some formal protection regime — and the Legal Amazon, which holds a large share of those areas, corresponds to 59% of national territory. Few countries in the world keep such a large proportion of their own territory under formal protection, a territorial asset that is at once a strategic environmental resource and an enforcement challenge on a continental scale.

The Cerrado has lost more forest than the Amazon

In absolute deforested area, the Cerrado surpasses both the Atlantic Forest and the Amazon itself — a finding that runs counter to the prevailing public perception, which associates deforestation almost exclusively with the Amazon rainforest. The Cerrado, with historically weaker protection rules than the Amazon, advanced as an agricultural frontier precisely because it drew less international and domestic scrutiny.

Protected areas work — but enforcement is weak

Where formal protection exists and is enforced, it works: fully protected conservation units register 80% lower deforestation than equivalent unprotected areas, and Indigenous lands show a similar protective effect. The problem isn't the design of protection policy — it's the real capacity to enforce it on the ground, especially in remote regions where state presence is sparse.

Quilombola communities without land titles

Concentrated mainly in the Northeast and Southeast, mapped Quilombola territories average around five thousand hectares — but frequently remain in dispute, without definitive titling recognized by the state. That legal limbo leaves entire communities vulnerable to land conflicts and pressure from land grabbers over territory that, by historical right, is already theirs.

Brazil's smallest unit of territorial analysis

The census tract is the smallest piece of IBGE's territorial puzzle — a subdivision that, in the 2022 Census, exceeds 450,000 units nationwide, nearly double the combined total of urban and rural tracts recorded in 2010. It's at this level of granularity that inequalities become visible that disappear once data is aggregated by municipality or state.

Metro areas grow, rural areas empty out

Between 2010 and 2022, the country's largest urban concentrations kept growing, with Belo Horizonte registering the fastest pace among the four largest. São Paulo alone already gathers 22 million people in its urban concentration area — more than the population of dozens of entire countries. It confirms a decades-long trend: rural Brazil is emptying out while the population concentrates in an ever-shrinking number of large urban centers.

Half a million square kilometers of internal border without a defined owner

More than 500 territorial disputes between municipalities remain active in the country, totaling roughly 500,000 km² of area pending definition — an extent comparable to entire European countries. These disputes concentrate in Tocantins, Mato Grosso, and Pará, states on the recent agricultural frontier, where administrative boundaries historically were never fully settled.

450,000 georeferenced polygons for spatial analysis

From biome down to census tract, the set of territorial meshes provided by the geobr project forms one of the country's most complete spatial databases — covering Brazil's 5,570 municipalities, every registered conservation unit and Indigenous land, and more than 450,000 individual census tracts. It's the data infrastructure that makes it possible to cross-reference any socioeconomic indicator with the exact territory where it occurs.

A third of Brazilian municipalities have low human development

Classifying Brazilian municipalities by HDI bracket, only 5% reach the very-high category, while 35% fall into the low or very-low brackets — the statistical portrait of what's commonly called "deep Brazil." The distribution isn't a balanced bell curve around the mean: it's a heavy tail of municipalities that, decades after the index itself was created, still haven't reached basic levels of human development.

Explanatory hypotheses

International pressure over Amazon deforestation helped create an extensive network of formally protected areas, but not always with effective enforcement capacity on the ground — hence the persistence of land grabbing in remote regions, where state presence is insufficient to make protection-on-paper hold up. Population concentration in a handful of metro areas — São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, and Belo Horizonte together total tens of millions of residents — shows that urban Brazil is, in practice, a country of few large centers. And territorial disputes between municipalities reveal that, across significant parts of national territory, the administrative border itself was never definitively settled — territory without a clear owner tends to become fertile ground for land grabbing.

Policy implications

Strengthening enforcement in conservation units and Indigenous lands could reduce the deforestation that persists today even in formally protected areas. Land regularization could guarantee territorial rights to communities that currently live under permanent legal insecurity. Effective demarcation of Quilombola and Indigenous lands could protect traditional populations from recurring land conflicts. Resolving pending municipal disputes could end a chronic source of legal uncertainty over half a million square kilometers of territory. And wider use of geobr's territorial meshes for spatial analysis could help public authorities identify, with territorial precision, the priority areas for public-policy investment.
