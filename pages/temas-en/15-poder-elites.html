Power, Elites & Social Reproduction

The Chamber of Deputies elected in 2026 is 21% women and 73% white; the median declared wealth of those elected is R$ 1.4 million, seventeen times that of the candidates who lost.

For every election, Brazil's Superior Electoral Court (TSE) records who ran, with party, gender, race, age and the list of declared assets, plus the result and every real that went into the campaigns. The party membership register counts 16.7 million members in good standing. With the 2026 election counted, the same funnel can be followed from the electorate to the Chamber of Deputies. At each step the makeup drifts further from the population: in gender, in race and, above all, in wealth.

The 2026 Chamber: 109 women in 513 seats

In 2026 Brazil elected 109 women to the federal Chamber, 21.2% of the house. It is the highest number in the recent series: 77 in 2018 and 91 in 2022. Progress is slow, though. At about 16 seats per election, it would take ten more elections to reach the 271 seats that would mirror an electorate that is 52.8% female, around 2066.

In eight years the women's caucus grew from 77 to 109 seats, but the Chamber remains four-fifths male in an electorate that is majority female.

The funnel: where women drop out

Women are a majority of the electorate and nearly half of party members. The drop happens in two steps. The first is candidacy: the law requires each party to field at least 30% women, and in 2026 they were 36.8% of federal deputy slates. The second, steeper step is the election itself, where the share falls from 37% to 21%. The quota puts the name on the ballot, but it does not deliver the vote.

Race: a 73% white Chamber in a 44% white country

By the candidates' own declaration to the TSE, 374 of the 513 deputies elected in 2026 are white (72.9%), 106 are brown, or pardo (20.7%), and 29 are Black (5.7%). In IBGE's 2022 Census the population is 45.3% pardo, 43.5% white and 10.2% Black. Pardo and Black Brazilians, the majority of the population, hold just over a quarter of the seats, and the proportion has barely moved since 2018.

Wealth: winners declare seventeen times more

Every candidate files a list of assets with the TSE. Among the 7,803 federal deputy candidates in 2026, the 513 winners have a median net worth of R$ 1.39 million. The 7,290 who lost have a median of R$ 83 thousand, counting those who declared no assets as zero. Of the winners, 306 (60%) declared R$ 1 million or more, against 1,133 (16%) of the losers.

Who funds campaigns: public money flows through the parties

Corporate donations to campaigns have been banned since 2015. In 2022, the latest election with closed accounts, candidates received R$ 6.63 billion. Of that, 81% (R$ 5.39 billion) came from parties, which mainly pass on the Special Campaign Financing Fund: R$ 4.90 billion from that source alone. Individual donations added up to R$ 826 million (12.5%), and candidates' own money to R$ 196 million. In practice, the party leadership decides how much each candidate gets.

Parties: six hold 70% of the seats

In 2026, 28 parties ran candidates for the federal Chamber and 20 elected at least one. The distribution is concentrated, though. PL elected 121 deputies and PT 70. Adding União, PSD, Republicanos and PP, the six largest hold 362 of the 513 seats (70.6%).

Re-election: 57% of the 2026 Chamber was already there

Of the 513 deputies elected in 2026, 294 (57.3%) had also been elected federal deputies in 2022, matched by voter ID. In 2022, at least 277 of those elected (54%) came from the previous legislature; that figure is a floor, because eight 2022 winners have no CPF on record. Each election renews about 43% to 46% of the seats, and part of that renewal is former deputies, councillors and mayors switching offices.

Powerful crossings

  • Gender × electoral funnel: women are 53% of voters, 37% of candidates and 21% of those elected to the Chamber in 2026.
  • Race × Chamber: 73% of deputies elected in 2026 declare themselves white, against 43.5% of the population.
  • Wealth × election: the winners' median (R$ 1.39 million) is 17 times that of the losers.
  • Party × public money: 81% of 2022 campaign revenue came from parties, mostly from the Special Fund.
  • Party × seats: six parties took 70.6% of the Chamber in 2026.
  • Re-election × renewal: 57% of those elected in 2026 were already federal deputies.
  • Membership × gender: women are 46% of the 16.7 million party members, but the share shrinks at every stage of the funnel.

Explanatory hypotheses

Social reproduction theory explains part of the picture: those already in the political elite tend to perpetuate their position, through advantages in resources or networks. Since corporate donations were banned, the main channel of that power has moved. Campaign money is now mostly public and is allocated by party leaderships, so the decisive contest happens inside the party, before the ballot. Personal wealth counts twice: it funds the pre-campaign and signals to the party who is a viable candidate. Together, these explain why the candidacy quota raised the number of women on slates far more than in seats, and why more than half the Chamber is re-elected.

Policy implications

Candidacy quotas alone have limited effect. The rule earmarking at least 30% of the Special Fund for women candidates needs to be monitored per candidate, not only per party total. Transparent criteria for sharing the fund within each party would reduce the leadership's power to pick winners in advance. Reserved seats, used in other countries, target the step where women lose most: the election itself. For racial under-representation, the TSE's decision to allocate funds and airtime proportionally to Black candidates also needs to be checked candidacy by candidacy.
