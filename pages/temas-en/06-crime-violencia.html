Crime, Violence & Public Security

A Black man between 15 and 29 has eight times the chance of dying from police intervention than a white man the same age — and 80% of firearm deaths hit young people.

The Mortality Information System (SIM), run by the Ministry of Health, records the official cause of every death in Brazil — including age, race, and circumstance of death. Public-security statistics from Rio de Janeiro's Institute of Public Security (ISP) complement that picture with territorial crime data, month by month, area by area. Together, the two panels sketch a Brazilian lethal violence that is young, is racial, and is, above all, geographically concentrated wherever the state is absent.

Firearm deaths: an entire generation at risk

In a single year, more than 26,000 young Brazilians died from causes tied to firearms — assault, events of undetermined intent, accidental exposure, and self-harm combined. That's the size of a mid-sized city wiped out in twelve months, concentrated almost entirely among young people aged 15 to 29, the population's most productive and promising age bracket.

Why do white people die more from firearms?

Firearm-assault records contradict the most widely publicized narrative on race and lethal violence: in absolute numbers, white people show more registered deaths than mixed-race people. The result is strange enough to cast suspicion on the registry itself — the more plausible explanation isn't that violence spares mixed-race people, but that part of their deaths are recorded with incorrect, incomplete, or off-the-books race data.

COVID-19 killed more than all lethal violence combined

In 2021, the pandemic killed more Brazilians than every external cause of death combined — homicides, accidents, suicides, and assorted assaults. It's a reminder of scale: even in a country with alarming rates of lethal violence, a poorly managed health crisis can, in a matter of months, outstrip what violence accumulates over years.

Firearm deaths by age

Isolating just firearm assaults, the age pattern is unambiguous: eight in ten victims are between 15 and 29. Lethal firearm violence in Brazil isn't a phenomenon distributed across the population — it's concentrated almost entirely in a single generation, the one that should be entering the labor market and starting families.

SINAN: violence reports by type

When you look at all types of violence reported to the health system, not just the homicides that make the news, domestic violence stands out as the most frequent. It's a violence that happens behind closed doors, against women and children, and remains largely invisible in the public debate on security.

Police lethality: young Black men in the crosshairs

The rate of deaths from police intervention among Black men aged 15 to 29 is eight times higher than the rate among white men of the same age bracket. It isn't a marginal difference — it's a systematic pattern that makes skin color one of the leading predictors of death at the state's own hands.

Crime in Rio de Janeiro: the territorial pattern

ISP statistics for Rio de Janeiro show, with territorial clarity, what happens when the state withdraws from an area: the homicide rate and robbery numbers spike. Where policing is absent, the homicide rate exceeds 50 per 100,000 residents; in areas with more present, community-integrated policing, the rate drops to a fraction of that.

Violence against women: the SINAN portrait

Adding up reports of physical, psychological, sexual, and torture-related violence against women, you get more than 320,000 records a year — a figure that, even as just the reported share, is already large enough to constitute a silent epidemic inside Brazilian homes.

Lethal violence against LGBTQI+ people: the data the state does not collect

No official Brazilian system records the sexual orientation or gender identity of homicide victims — neither police reports nor the mortality system has the field. The only continuous national count is produced by a civil society organisation, Grupo Gay da Bahia, from press and social media reports, and therefore measures reported homicides, not homicides that occurred: a floor, not a total. With that caveat, the series peaks at 445 deaths in 2017 and falls to 329 in 2019. The breakdown by group exposes the harshest disproportion: in 2020, trans and travesti people accounted for 164 of identified deaths against 51 gay men — an inversion of 2019, when gay men were 52.9% and trans/travesti people 36.8%. Whether the shift reflects a real displacement of violence or improved victim identification is indistinguishable with the available data, which is itself the finding: the absence of state collection makes it impossible to separate trend from artefact.

Powerful cross-references

  • Firearm × race: white people show up with more registered deaths than mixed-race people.
  • Age × violence: 80% of firearm deaths hit young people aged 15 to 29.
  • COVID-19 × vulnerable groups: the pandemic killed 424,000 people, disproportionately the poor.
  • Police × race: a Black man has eight times the chance of dying from police lethality.
  • Domestic violence × gender: 40% of reports involve women as victims.
  • State × violence: state absence is associated with up to ten times more homicides.
  • SINAN × underreporting: 320,000 reports a year, with estimates that real incidence is far higher.
  • LGBTQI+ × Missing data: no official system records victim identity; the only figure comes from an NGO.

Explanatory hypotheses

Firearm violence can be explained by ease of access: Brazil has one of the largest per-capita fleets of firearms in circulation in the world, legal and illegal. The connection to race suggests likely underreporting of mixed-race deaths, distorting the official portrait of lethal violence by color. Minimal-state theory helps explain the persistence of ineffective security policies in vulnerable areas. And the predominance of domestic violence as the leading cause of reports reveals that, for many Brazilian women and children, the most immediate danger is inside the home, not on the street.

Policy implications

Effective disarmament, with real enforcement of the flow of weapons, has the potential to measurably reduce lethal violence. Arms control in cooperation with Mercosur countries could reduce the cross-border flow of illegal weapons. Preventing COVID-19 deaths among poor populations requires specific, not just generic, health policies. Women's assistance units need to be expanded, with 24-hour operation, across the entire territory. Community policing in areas currently without state presence could reduce lethality in those territories. And urban disarmament policies remain urgent in the face of a number that shouldn't be normalized: firearms kill more than 26,000 people a year in the country.
