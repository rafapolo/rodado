Politics, Representation & Electoral Behavior

Only 4.4% of federal representatives are women, and a third of congressional candidates are lawyers or business owners — Brazil's formal politics is, above all, a self-reproducing elite.

The candidate records from the Superior Electoral Court (TSE) cover every election held in the country, with information on gender, race, education, occupation, and party for every contender. Municipal-level results complete the picture with real vote counts, and the Chamber of Deputies' open data adds biography and career trajectory for those who actually reach federal power. Cross-referenced, these three registries show that Brazilian politics doesn't reflect the population it governs — it reflects a narrow, well-defined slice of it: overwhelmingly male, white, highly educated, and tied to the legal and business professions.

TSE 2022: candidates concentrated in a few parties

Despite the enormous number of party labels registered in Brazil, actual candidacies concentrate in a handful of them. The six largest parties by number of candidates account for nearly a third of all candidacies registered in 2022 — a sign that Brazilian multipartyism is more apparent than real once you look at where candidacies actually organize themselves.

The Chamber: being a woman is the exception

Among everyone who has ever passed through the Chamber of Deputies in recent republican history, men are an overwhelming majority. Only 4.4% of records belong to women — one of the lowest rates of female representation in parliaments worldwide, in a country where women are the majority of the population.

Presidential elections: a growing electorate

From 2010 to 2022, the Brazilian electorate grew steadily — the same 5,570 municipalities keep voting election after election, with total turnout rising from just over 200 million to nearly 237 million votes. It's the largest direct election in the world after India, run with electronic voting logistics that continues without major failures in the official numbers.

Candidate education against the general population

While only one in five people in Brazil has completed higher education, two in three candidates for elected office have. The gap widens even further at the graduate-degree level. Whoever runs for public office in Brazil isn't a mirror of the population they'll govern — it's an educational elite competing for the votes of people who, for the most part, never had access to the same schooling.

Predominant occupation: lawyers and business owners

A third of all Brazilian candidates come from just two professions: law and business ownership. Teachers, the category that deals most directly with shaping the population, show up with a tiny fraction of representation at the ballot box — an imbalance that helps explain why certain issues enter and others leave the legislative debate.

Party membership: extreme concentration

Despite Brazil having dozens of registered parties, actual party membership is heavily concentrated in a handful of labels. Ten parties account for nearly half of all registered members in the country — a party oligopoly that contradicts the image of extreme fragmentation associated with the Brazilian political system.

Campaign spending: what it costs to be a candidate

Running for federal legislative office in Brazil costs, on average, more than a million reais. Even the most accessible office, city councilor, requires average spending far above the reach of most of the working population. Money isn't just a competitive advantage in Brazilian politics: it's, in practice, an entry requirement.

Powerful cross-references

  • Race × candidacy: Black candidates are 25% of candidates against 56% of the population.
  • Gender × office: women are 4.4% of representatives against 52% of the population.
  • Education × elite: candidates form an educational elite — 65% with higher education against 20% of the general population.
  • Occupation × class: 33% are lawyers or business owners against 5% manual laborers.
  • Party × power: ten parties concentrate 45% of members — a party oligopoly.
  • Money × candidacy: R$ 1.2 million to run for federal representative excludes low-income candidates.

Explanatory hypotheses

Female underrepresentation can be explained by the glass-ceiling hypothesis: party and family norms limit women's candidacy from nomination all the way through financing. The connection to money reinforces that barrier — female candidates receive, on average, much lower campaign revenue than male candidates. Differential-access theory explains the mechanism behind it: male networks dominate the nomination of competitive names within parties. The overrepresentation of lawyers and business owners, meanwhile, reveals a pattern of captured democracy, in which people with more financial and legal resources disproportionately dominate the very process of competing for power.

Policy implications

Gender quotas have limited effect when not accompanied by equal campaign financing. Stricter limits on corporate donations could help democratize access to electoral competition. Collective candidacies, already tested in some municipal elections, have the potential to reduce the cost of entry into politics. Free transportation and logistical support for female candidates could increase women's participation at the ballot box. And public campaign financing, if well regulated, could reduce the weight of private money as a filter on who gets to run.
