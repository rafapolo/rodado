# Strong findings × online coverage

Filter of [`achados_fortes.md`](achados_fortes.md) with two extra questions per row — **is this
phenomenon already known on the internet?** and **why does it matter?** Editorial curation
before deciding what becomes `pages/analises/`, not a check of scientific priority. ~35 searches
(`WebSearch`, 2026-09-07) for the *general phenomenon*, not the exact correlation — what's being
checked is whether the pattern already circulates.

## Negative findings — when the null is the news

A different scale from the table above: the badge here doesn't measure whether the *finding*
is covered (by definition, an internal null has no published parallel), but whether the
**belief it contradicts** is documented — the more official the contradicted belief, the
stronger the null's news value.

`achados_fortes.md` itself already names a pattern and tracks it across seven independent
findings, scattered through the list and each read in isolation as a one-off case:
**"the rule doesn't bite"** — a formal control mechanism (fiscal, procurement, credit,
cultural) that exists *explicitly* to filter risk, and in practice filters nothing. Together,
these aren't seven average findings — they're a single strong finding: the same structural
failure shows up in seven domains that have no administrative reason to share a cause.

| # | Badge | Finding | Relevance | Is the belief documented? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **T68-3** | 🔴 | CAUC (fiscal pending items) × grant-agreement success: the National Treasury/FNP state that pending items **block** transfers; partial r ≈ −0.001 — the earmark (B1) is what actually decides | Shows that the CAUC fiscal filter is decorative — political brokering, not fiscal regularity, is what gets grant agreements approved; questions the legitimacy of the block itself. An isolated "no effect" wouldn't get press coverage | Yes — it's CAUC's own official justification |
| **G7** | 🔴 | CAPAG (municipal credit-capacity rating) × actual debt issuance: the National Treasury says the rating exists to signal credit risk; partial r = −0.004 controlling for income — the apparent gradient is disguised GDP per capita | An indicator used by markets/investors to assess municipal credit risk doesn't predict actual indebtedness — risk of financial decisions resting on a number with no predictive power. The Treasury itself already notes CAPAG "is an estimate, not a finding of the actual situation" | Yes — the purpose is declared by the Treasury itself |
| **D7 / F1** | 🔴 | PGFN debtors × municipal tenders/payments: fiscal regularity is a formal prerequisite; R$241.7 bn in federal contracts and 22.7% of municipal payment value (12.2% of creditors) go to debtors anyway | R$241.7 bn at stake — a large-scale failure of the country's main fiscal-regularity filter, big enough to be a fiscal-impact finding, not just a statistical one | Yes — it's written into the Procurement Law |
| **D9 / K2 / F5** | 🔴 | CEIS/CNEP (sanctions registry) × ability to contract: the regulation says sanctioned entities are barred; R$18.4 bn paid to sanctioned entities anyway, and sanctioned entities convert 17–24× more among Rouanet cultural-law sponsors | The registry meant to bar entities from the public market doesn't block access to contracts or cultural sponsorship — a systemic failure of the main anti-corruption blacklist instrument | Yes — the ban is the registry's reason for existing |
| **F6** | 🟡 | Serious CGU audit findings × municipal poverty: the implicit assumption is that poorer municipalities should show more serious findings; raw +0.37 → partial +0.08 | The variable used to measure irregularity risk is, in practice, an artifact of who got selected for audit — risk of anti-corruption policy targeting the wrong municipality | An implicit belief, not declared by any agency |
| **C3 / D19 / J5** | 🟡 | Mandatory-notification data (violence, dengue) × actual incidence: notification series are treated as measuring the phenomenon; they predict access to services far more than actual outcomes — J5: 4.4× more predictive of notification than of hospitalization | Series used as an epidemiological thermometer mostly measure access to services — risk of public policy targeting the wrong place, believing it measures the phenomenon itself | A common-use belief in research, rarely questioned explicitly |
| **I2** | 🟡 | CFEM (mining royalty) shocks × employment/fiscal health: conventional wisdom predicts a "boom" brings more jobs and fiscal slack; partial r +0.04 (CAGED) and −0.002 (CAUC) | Debunks the mining "boom" narrative — CFEM royalties generate no detectable formal employment or municipal fiscal slack | A diffuse belief, not institutional |
| **h3-extra** | — | Sanctioned entities (CEIS) are 220× more likely (Mantel-Haenszel, CNAE×age-stratified) to also be federal outsourcing contractors; CNEP-sanctioned entities, 158× | The inverse and more serious case of the other seven: the sanctions registry isn't neutral, it **concentrates** risk exactly where it should block it | Not applicable — here the control fails in reverse, concentrating instead of filtering |

**The meta-pattern is the story.** Seven formal control mechanisms that exist to filter
risk, and none of them filter — that, on its own, is stronger as a story than any one of
the seven alone. "None of Brazil's public-sector integrity locks actually lock" is the
headline the list points to, one that no individual item told on its own — and is,
plausibly, the most publishable finding in all of `achados_fortes.md`. The eighth case
(h3-extra) is the strongest of all: unlike the other seven — null or near-null — here the
sanctions registry doesn't just fail to filter, it concentrates.

The 🟢 items are the candidates for a genuinely new angle, but an empty search isn't
confirmation — deeper checking is warranted (Google Scholar, newspaper archives, direct

outside Google's index don't show up in any search, so a 🟢 item might already be covered
somewhere the scan didn't reach.
